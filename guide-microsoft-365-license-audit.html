What a Microsoft 365 License Audit Found at a Small Medical Practice, by Danielle Lemons

At DirecTech Connect in Nashville, we handle phones, IT and websites for small businesses. I recently audited the Microsoft 365 bill for a small medical practice whose licenses had been set up by another IT provider. It's a good picture of where the money goes.

What the practice was paying for

The practice paid $159.60 a month through a reseller for 7 licenses: 4 Business Premium, 1 Business Standard and 2 Business Basic. On top of that were two Entra ID P2 security add-ons at $22.80 a month, one of them added the week before I looked, and a separate spam filter, even though Business Premium already includes email protection.

What it actually needed

The practice ran on 3 shared email addresses. Shared mailboxes don't need a license in Microsoft 365, but the setup paid for seats as if every address were a person. Once we listed who actually signs in and who actually uses desktop Word and Excel, only one person needed a full license. The other four needed email only, which Microsoft sells for about $4 a month per person. My estimate is that the bill can drop from $159.60 to roughly $50 to $60 a month, without anyone losing a single email.

A simple way to find your true cost per user

Take the total monthly bill, including every add-on, backup and filtering line, and divide it by the number of people who actually sign in. Then compare that with what each person uses. If someone only reads email in a browser and on a phone, they shouldn't be on a premium plan.

What to do before your renewal

  1. Get the invoice itemized by license type and add-on, not just the total.
  2. List every person, the address they sign in with and whether they open desktop Office apps.
  3. Convert shared addresses like info@ or billing@ to shared mailboxes, which are free.
  4. Ask whoever manages the account what each add-on does. If no one can answer, check it, and remove it only after you confirm nothing depends on it.
  5. Keep compliance in mind. For a medical practice, staying on Microsoft for email mattered because Microsoft's HIPAA agreement covers it, and anyone handling patient records still gets their own sign-in rather than sharing one.

The rule that applies to every add-on

If you can't name who uses it, you probably shouldn't be paying for it.
